About the research

Thanks for taking a look at my poster. I kept the references off the printed page so there would be more room for the charts and research findings. Here you can find the studies I reviewed, where the chart figures came from, and how I calculated the percentages.

This project looks at a simple question: does bringing chip manufacturing back to the United States make the supply chain more resilient, or do we also need supplier diversification, better information sharing, and tools that help companies spot problems earlier?

About the methods: This is a literature review and comparison of published work. I did not run the trade-network simulations or test an AI forecasting model myself. I have kept the published results, projections, and my own interpretations separate below.

Where the charts came from

1. U.S. manufacturing capacity: 2022 and projected 2032

The reshoring chart is based on a 2024 report from the Semiconductor Industry Association (SIA) and Boston Consulting Group (BCG). It compares the U.S. share of global wafer fabrication capacity in two different categories.

U.S. share of global capacity2022 baseline2032 forecast
All wafer fabrication10%14%
Advanced logic (below 10 nm)0%28%

How to read it: The two rows describe different categories and should not be added together. The 2032 percentages are forecasts published in 2024, not results that have already happened. See the SIA report summary (Figure 3).

2. Trade-network vulnerability: simulated disruptions

The second chart comes from Li et al. (2025), Table 6. The paper models three parts of the semiconductor trade network using 2021 data. It compares random removal of network nodes with targeted removal of more influential nodes.

Trade segmentNetwork nodesRandom removalsTargeted removalsRandom shareTargeted share
Raw materials5839667.2%10.3%
Equipment4833668.8%12.5%
Finished components9667969.8%9.4%

The percentages in my poster are calculated as nodes removed ÷ total network nodes × 100, rounded to one decimal place. In the study, collapse means less than 10% of the original trade volume remains. These are simulated outcomes, not a prediction of the probability or economic cost of a real disruption. Notes on the other visuals

Production chain

The materials, equipment, fabrication, packaging, and end-use diagram is a simplified overview. It helps show why moving fabrication alone does not relocate every supplier or production stage. Kamakura (2022) and Xiong et al. (2025) informed this part of the review.

COVID-19 shortage

The shortage flow diagram summarizes the chain of events discussed by Ramani, Ghosh, and Sodhi (2022), especially the interaction of supply constraints, shifting demand, and automotive production. It is a conceptual illustration, not a measured time-series chart.

Combining strategies

The physical-strategies and data-and-decisions comparison is my summary of the literature. Moktadir and Ren (2024), Ramirez and Le (2025), Smyth et al. (2024), and Zamani et al. (2023) helped shape the comparison.

AI's role

The research suggests that forecasting, early warnings, and better information sharing could support decisions. This is not evidence that any specific AI system has already improved semiconductor resilience by a measured amount.
